Savannah’s historic district and the expanding Southside don’t just differ in architecture — they sit on surprisingly different ground. The older downtown rests on relatively shallow Pleistocene sands and silts, while areas south of DeRenne Avenue often encounter thicker layers of compressible clay that amplify ground motion in ways that a blanket code coefficient can’t capture. We got involved in seismic microzonation here after seeing engineers apply uniform site factors across the entire Chatham County map, missing the sharp transition between NEHRP Site Class D and E that runs through parts of Pooler and Georgetown. A detailed MASW survey combined with SPT drilling lets us map shear-wave velocity profiles street by street, so the structural team isn’t guessing about site amplification. This type of work matters especially where the Floridan aquifer is shallow — high groundwater in Savannah routinely skews VS30 estimates when only surface methods are used.

Savannah’s shallow groundwater and layered coastal sediments create a site response puzzle that default code categories simply weren’t built to solve.

Process and scope

The geology under Savannah is dominated by Quaternary and Tertiary deposits of the Atlantic Coastal Plain, with a distinct sequence of loose sands, stiff overconsolidated clays, and occasional marl layers that influence how seismic waves propagate. What we see in the field is a VS30 range roughly between 180 m/s in the softer creek-bottom fills near the Vernon River and over 350 m/s on the Pleistocene terraces west of I-95. This variation, driven by changes in grain size and cementation, is exactly why microzonation requires direct measurement rather than proxy-based correlations. Our procedure starts with a desktop review of existing grain-size analysis logs and USGS Quaternary maps, then layers in active-source surface wave testing and downhole velocity checks to build a three-dimensional site response model. Where low-velocity lenses appear at depths between 15 and 40 feet — a common situation along the Savannah River corridor — we integrate liquefaction susceptibility screening under the NCEER framework to flag zones where cyclic softening could compromise shallow footings. The final deliverable is a set of site-specific response spectra and amplification factors that align with ASCE 7-22 Chapter 21, giving the design team something far more defensible than a default Site D assumption.

Local ground factors

In Savannah, Georgia, the primary hazard often isn't the size of the design earthquake; instead, it's the discrepancy between the actual ground response of a site and the default values taken from a county-wide hazard map. The soft alluvial clays filling old river channels and marshlands near the Savannah National Wildlife Refuge tend to amplify ground motion at periods matching two- and three-story structures—a detail only revealed through a microzonation study that maps the thickness and shear-wave velocity of those deposits. Foundation designs that overlook this amplification may later encounter interstory drift issues years down the line. Additionally, contractors frequently underestimate how quickly groundwater can rise after heavy rainfall, altering effective stress and potentially shifting a site from Class D to Class E behavior on a seasonal basis. A microzonation relying solely on dry-season data fails to capture this reality, and the resulting spectral accelerations won't fully represent the demands on a project intended to last 50 years.

Reference standards

Relevant standards include ASCE 7-22 Chapter 21 for site-specific ground motion procedures, ASTM D4428/D4428M for crosshole seismic testing, NEHRP recommended provisions for site classification, the NCEER/NSF 1997 guidelines (updated by Youd-Idriss 2001) for liquefaction resistance of soils, and ASTM D1586 for the standard penetration test.

Other technical services

01

Surface Wave and Downhole Velocity Testing

On each lot, we conduct active MASW lines in multiple orientations and, where lot size permits, supplement them with passive microtremor recordings. In areas with shallow groundwater—such as neighborhoods east of Truman Parkway—we add downhole velocity checks to calibrate the surface-wave dispersion curves. This yields a VS profile with sufficient resolution to detect velocity inversions, like soft clay beneath stiff sand, which would otherwise go unnoticed and could lead to an underestimation of site amplification.

02

Geotechnical Integration and Spectral Mapping

Velocity data alone is insufficient; every VS profile is linked to SPT blow counts, grain-size distributions, and Atterberg limits from nearby borings to ensure that amplification factors are based on actual soil behavior. This integrated dataset feeds into one-dimensional equivalent-linear site response analyses, producing maps of SDS and SD1 across the project footprint. For larger tracts, we deliver GIS-ready shapefiles that civil and structural teams can directly overlay on their grading and foundation plans.

Q&A

At what project scale does seismic microzonation become necessary in Savannah?

Typically, microzonation is warranted when a project spans several acres with varying soil conditions, or when essential facilities like hospitals and emergency response centers are being designed. In Savannah, the transition from Pleistocene terraces to Holocene floodplain deposits can occur over a few hundred feet, so any development straddling two NEHRP site classes benefits from microzonation. Even smaller infill projects in the historic district sometimes request it when the structural engineer aims to justify a site-specific reduction in design spectral accelerations.

How much does a seismic microzonation study cost in the Savannah area?

For most sites in Chatham County, the total cost ranges from US$4,310 to US$16,540, depending on the number of MASW lines and calibration borings required, and whether passive array measurements are necessary for deeper velocity profiles. Larger parcels with multiple soil transitions trend toward the upper end because they demand denser spatial coverage to accurately map changes in site class.

Does Savannah’s shallow groundwater affect the VS30 measurements?

Yes, and it is one of the first factors we consider. Saturated sands and silts can transmit shear waves faster than the same material in a dry state, but the effect varies across frequencies. We employ downhole velocity checks and, when available, compare results with CPT pore pressure dissipation tests to separate true stiffness changes from saturation effects, ensuring the VS30 value reflects the soil skeleton rather than just the water table.

What earthquake sources drive the seismic hazard for Savannah?

The hazard is dominated by two source types: the Charleston seismic zone, about 100 miles up the coast, which produced the 1886 earthquake, and the smaller but closer faults in the Piedmont and Coastal Plain that generate moderate events. Microzonation for Savannah must therefore consider both the long-period energy from a Charleston-type event and the short-period shaking from a local moderate quake, because the soft coastal soils respond quite differently to each.
