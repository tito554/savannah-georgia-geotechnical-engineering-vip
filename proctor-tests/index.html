ASTM D698 and ASTM D1557 define the standard for compaction control across the United States, but in Savannah, the local geology adds a layer of complexity that generic lab reports often miss. The city sits on Pleistocene and Holocene deposits of the Atlantic Coastal Plain, where clean sands, silty sands, and fat clays can alternate within a single project site. When we run a Proctor test on material from Pooler or the marsh-edge developments near the Savannah River, the moisture-density curve doesn't just tell you a number—it reveals how the soil will respond to the humidity cycles and high water table that define construction in Chatham County. Before a contractor places fill for a warehouse pad off Jimmy DeLoach Parkway or compacts subgrade for a runway expansion at Savannah/Hilton Head International, knowing the maximum dry density and optimum moisture content is non-negotiable. We typically pair the sand cone density test with the laboratory Proctor to verify field compaction reaches at least 95% of the standard maximum, and for deeper strata characterization, the SPT drilling program provides the samples we use to select representative material for the moisture-density relationship.

A one percent deviation from optimum moisture in Savannah's silty sands can drop the field dry density by three to five pounds per cubic foot—and that difference shows up in differential settlement within the first two wet seasons.

Process and scope

Savannah's subtropical climate, with summer thunderstorms and a shallow groundwater table that often sits less than six feet below grade, means that moisture conditioning of fill is a constant battle on site. The Standard Proctor (ASTM D698, 12,400 ft-lbf/ft³ compactive effort) is typically specified for residential subdivisions and light commercial pads, where the native silty sands can be sensitive to overcompaction. The Modified Proctor (ASTM D1557, 56,000 ft-lbf/ft³) applies to heavier structural fill beneath bridge approaches on the Truman Parkway or industrial floors at the port logistics centers. We run both methods in our Savannah lab using material extracted from Shelby tubes or bulk samples taken during the test pits investigation phase. The key parameter for local engineers is the relationship between the percent compaction achieved and the soil's as-compacted hydraulic conductivity—a poorly compacted clay liner in a Savannah stormwater pond can fail at the interface where the Hawthorn Formation confining layer is breached.
  • Mold size selection follows AASHTO T-99 or T-180 based on percent retained on No. 4 sieve.
  • Moisture content is determined by oven-drying at 110±5°C per ASTM D2216, with at least four points defining the compaction curve.
  • For soils with more than 30% retained on the 3/4-inch sieve, we apply the rock correction procedure per ASTM D4718.
  • Zero air voids curve is plotted on every report to validate the saturation line at the specific gravity measured per ASTM D854.

Local ground factors

In Savannah, contractors often fail to adjust compaction specifications when the natural moisture content significantly exceeds optimum. For low-lying zones south of DeRenne Avenue, where groundwater sits just three feet below the surface, we advise laboratory moisture conditioning trials and a specification permitting reduced compactive effort on the initial lift over natural subgrade instead of requiring a standard that is unattainable without lime treatment or prolonged drying. The Pleistocene terraces traversing the city can transition from a clean SP sand to a plastic SC clay within a lateral distance of one hundred feet. Another prevalent oversight is assuming borrow material from a single source matches the Proctor curve developed for a different lift or site area. If a field density technician uses a nuclear gauge against a Proctor that does not represent the material being compacted, the reported percent compaction becomes meaningless, and the engineer of record might approve fill that will undergo uneven settling after the first heavy rain.

Reference standards

Relevant compaction standards for Savannah projects include ASTM D698-12(2021) and D1557-12(2021) for standard and modified efforts, ASTM D4718-87(2007) for oversize particle correction, ASTM D2216-19 for moisture content determination, plus AASHTO T 99-22 and T 180-22 as specified by Georgia DOT for roadway embankments.

Other technical services

01

Standard and Modified Proctor Testing

Using representative bulk samples of fill or subgrade from your Savannah site, we produce compaction curves for both standard and modified efforts. Reported results comprise the zero air voids curve, optimum moisture content, maximum dry density at full saturation, and rock correction if needed. Field Density Correlation and Material Verification

If nuclear gauge results fall short of specification, we conduct either a one-point Proctor check or a full curve on the problematic material to verify the reference Proctor's validity. Q&A

What is the typical cost for a Standard Proctor test on a Savannah project?

Pricing for a standard Proctor (ASTM D698) on one soil sample is between US$100 and US$140, contingent on the need for rock correction. A modified Proctor (ASTM D1557) costs US$140 to US$190 per sample. Discounted volume rates are available for multiple curves on the same project, which is typical for large site developments with changing borrow sources.

How do I know whether to specify the Standard or Modified Proctor for my fill?

Selection of compaction effort hinges on structural load and project specifications. In Savannah, the Standard Proctor (12,400 ft-lbf/ft³) is commonly used for residential slabs, landscaping fills, and utility trench backfill under light loads. The Modified Proctor (56,000 ft-lbf/ft³) is reserved for structural fill under foundations, bridge approach embankments, and heavy industrial floors—applications where the geotechnical report mandates higher density to control settlement under long-term loading.

How much material do you need to run a Proctor test?

For a Standard Proctor, around 45 to 50 pounds of representative soil is needed; for a Modified Proctor, about 65 to 70 pounds, particularly when the material includes gravel needing rock correction. Samples must be sealed in plastic bags right after excavation to retain natural moisture, and we advise collecting them from the same lift and source as the fill that will be placed.

Can you run a Proctor on soil that already has lime or cement mixed in?

Chemically stabilized soils are regularly tested in our lab. We follow ASTM D558 for soil-cement and ASTM D3551 for lime-treated soils; the compaction curve is time-sensitive due to immediate onset of hydration. We work with the contractor to ensure the sample arrives within the mix design's working window, and we provide the maximum dry density and optimum moisture content for the stabilized mix at the designated curing time.
